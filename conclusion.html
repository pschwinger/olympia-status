CONCLUSION

What this pilot showed

Digitised collections can carry a filmTwenty-three museum objects, the real digitisations bound into the frames, five chapters from the rediscovery of Olympia to the present day, one dress from Antwerp to prove the method travels.
Provenance can survive generationEvery shot states what it claims, on whose authority, and what the record does not say. Where a model invented, the gate caught it and the failed take stays published beside the fix. That is the answer to “you are going to invent things about our collection.”
Measured work compoundsBeliefs written before spending, graded after; mistakes logged with preventions; rules born from failures and reused the same day across practices. The second scene was cheaper and safer than the first because the first was measured.

How this helps you

Your metadata was the ground truthThe institutions’ own records set what each object is and what may be said about it. The films inherited that, and nothing else.
We checked before we producedRules, references and predictions were written and verified before credits were spent. The money went into generations that could pass, not into re-rolls.
The models improved, and we say soPart of the drop in failures is the technology itself getting better between the first tests and the last films. The record does not claim that for the method.
The pipeline made the differenceCombining many assets, objects, references, sheets and shots, into one finished product came out cheaper, faster and more accurate because every hand-off was specified, measured and kept.

What it means for a museum

A film whose every frame can be asked where does this come from, and answered. Objects that appear as themselves, with their own markings and their own wear, never re-drawn. A record that shows its failures, which is the only kind of record a curator can trust with a collection.
